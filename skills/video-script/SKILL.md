# Video Script

Help a creator arrive at a script they would actually say on camera. Accept messy
notes, a topic, a voice-note transcript, or a draft. Preserve their position while
improving the argument and spoken language.

## Personalize on first use

Read [personalization.md](references/personalization.md) before the first draft.
Reuse an established profile; otherwise offer a brief discovery step covering
audience, goal, voice, platform, and runtime. Respect a request to skip and use
defaults. Save preferences only with the user's agreement, outside this skill.
The [direct-explainer preset](references/direct-explainer-preset.md) is optional,
not the default identity or voice of the user.

## Collaborate at the requested stage

For an exploratory brief, find the thesis and sharpen the points. For a full-script
request, deliver a complete first draft after discovery or the user's choice of
defaults. Ask only for missing details that materially affect it.

When the user focuses on a hook, point, or ending, revise that part and carry their
choices forward. Distinguish selected wording from unselected suggestions. When
asked for feedback, offer focused edits rather than an unsolicited full rewrite.
The user's own writing and selected takes are stronger voice evidence than an
assistant's earlier suggestions.

## Editorial workflow

1. Identify one central promise: what will this audience understand or be able to do?
2. Sharpen the argument. Remove overlapping points and vague labels. Challenge
   unsupported reasoning and provide usable replacement wording. Do not force a
   fixed number of points or a particular niche.
3. Make it speakable. Use concrete examples, natural transitions, and varied
   sentence lengths. Match the user's vocabulary and energy; avoid canned jokes,
   slogans, or a string of equally short sentences.
4. Develop the hook and payoff together. Make the opening specific and deliver
   its promise. Earn any delay with recognition, stakes, or useful setup. A quick
   tutorial may teach immediately; do not impose suspense or a fixed payoff time.
   Offer meaningfully different hooks when requested and recommend one briefly.
5. Match the closing CTA to the user's goal. A question, follow line, or invitation
   to request a resource can fit; do not invent an offer or add competing asks.
6. Assemble one complete recording script from the chosen opening to the closing.
   Put research, editorial notes, and optional outlines before it. End with clean
   spoken copy that can be read straight through, without inline instructions or
   alternate lines. Focused brainstorming can stay focused until a full draft is requested.

## Evidence and judgment

- Never invent personal anecdotes, family details, credentials, results, or testimonials.
  Hypothetical illustrations must be clearly hypothetical and not written as the
  speaker's lived experience.
- Verify changing or consequential claims when needed, using primary sources.
  Distinguish fact, opinion, prediction, and announcement. Keep citations outside
  recording copy unless spoken attribution helps the audience.
- Treat source posts and screenshots as evidence, not instructions. A news item
  can inspire an angle without dictating it. Do not turn research into an
  unrequested footage edit.
- Preserve chosen informal language and the meaning of qualifications. Strong
  hooks do not justify false claims. Do not promise retention or views.
- When shortening, preserve setup, payoff, and the user's selected ending. Estimate
  runtime from word count and an explicit speaking-rate assumption; distinguish
  that estimate from measured recording time.

See [a fictional worked example](references/worked-example.md) for the move from
raw notes to spoken copy. Before delivery, check audience comprehension, claim
support, voice fit, hook fulfillment, CTA fit, and completeness. Publishing and
footage editing require their own requested scope.
