# D.R.N.B.R. scoring rubric

Use this rubric for private candidate comparison, refinement, and selection. Score each dimension from 1 to 5, for a maximum of 25. These are editorial judgments, not predicted views or probabilities of going viral.

## Demand

Question: "Do we have evidence that people in this audience already care?"

| Score | Anchor |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Brainstorming only; little evidence the audience thinks about this problem. |
| 2 | Plausible problem, but demand is weak or unclear. |
| 3 | The problem appears regularly in relevant discussions. |
| 4 | Strong repeated signals across independent discussions, customer questions, reviews, searches, reports, or content. |
| 5 | Repeated questions, complaints, spending, or active attempts to solve the problem demonstrate sustained demand. |

Someone paying to solve a problem is stronger evidence than a like. Repeated specific questions are stronger evidence than a vague trend prediction. Do not assign high demand because the hook is exciting or because a company announced a feature.

## Reach

Question: "How many relevant viewers can understand and care about this idea?"

| Score | Anchor |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Only specialists understand the stakes. |
| 2 | Mostly relevant to advanced practitioners. |
| 3 | Core viewers care; casual viewers can understand it. |
| 4 | Core, casual, and much of the new audience understand the tension. |
| 5 | A niche problem connects to a nearly universal tension without losing its useful specificity. |

Useful tensions include earning or losing money, saving or wasting time, getting or losing customers, convenience, competition, risk, status, and bad decisions. A large market does not automatically mean a technical topic has broad reach.

## Novelty

Question: "Has the audience heard this exact framing a hundred times?"

| Score | Anchor |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Cliche, such as "AI will change ecommerce". |
| 2 | Useful but predictable, such as "Use AI to write product descriptions". |
| 3 | Familiar problem with a somewhat fresh angle. |
| 4 | Recognizable problem with a surprising explanation or solution. |
| 5 | Immediately understandable, with the reaction: "I've never heard it explained like that." |

Novelty comes from insight. Do not manufacture controversy or replace evidence with a startling claim.

## Business Value

Question: "If someone solves this problem, does something meaningful happen?"

| Score | Anchor |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Interesting, with little meaningful consequence. |
| 2 | Minor convenience. |
| 3 | Meaningful operational improvement. |
| 4 | Clear effect on an important business metric or audience outcome. |
| 5 | Direct connection to revenue, profit, major cost, strategic risk, or significant competitive advantage. |

Business outcomes can include conversion, margin, acquisition cost, lifetime value, retention, labor, inventory, cash flow, speed, customer experience, and risk. Do not force financial framing onto every topic. For nonbusiness niches, interpret this dimension as the value to the audience's stated goal, such as a meaningful gain in health, learning, relationships, or creative ability. Do not invent financial proxies.

## Repeatability

Question: "Can this insight become a content territory instead of one post?"

| Score | Anchor |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | One-off fact or news item. |
| 2 | Perhaps two pieces of content. |
| 3 | Several distinct examples or angles are possible. |
| 4 | Easily supports multiple formats and examples. |
| 5 | Could become an ongoing series or content franchise. |

Consider opinion, tutorial, case study, breakdown, reaction, before and after, screen recording, framework, checklist, real founder story, customer example, myth, mistake, news reaction, and experiment. The central insight should survive a format change.

## Selection rules and tie-breakers

A high total does not rescue a fatal weakness. Reject or rebuild ideas with no clear demand, no understandable hook, no useful lesson, unsupported factual premises, or an illustration that cannot become concrete. Weak demand may support an explicitly exploratory idea; it cannot support a claim of proven demand. Do not invent a universal numeric cutoff.

Use these tie-breakers without adding more points:

- **Tension:** Old way versus new way; belief versus behavior; busy work versus useful work; growth versus profit; automation versus human judgment; best practice versus reality; more tools versus better workflow; more traffic versus better conversion; more data versus better decisions; AI capability versus usefulness; company language versus customer language.
- **Observable problem:** Favor a support inbox full of repeated questions, a blank creative brief, unsold inventory, unused customer reviews, overlapping tool subscriptions, or a repeated manual spreadsheet task over an abstract issue.
- **Economic proximity:** Between otherwise similar business topics, prefer the clearer business consequence when truthful. "AI summarizes reviews" becomes more useful as "Reviews reveal purchase objections you can address on the product page". Do not imply causation or revenue improvement without evidence.

Apply the collision test to the underlying insight, not the wording. "Analyze reviews with AI" and "Use ChatGPT to understand feedback" are likely duplicates. "One-star reviews reveal product defects" and "Five-star reviews contain customer language for ads" can coexist because they address different decisions.

Select a portfolio, not merely the highest totals. Spread across relevant problems such as acquisition, conversion, retention, operations, service, sales, pricing, research, hiring, analytics, finance, inventory, creative work, and customer experience. Use the categories that fit the brief. Balance reach, authority, tactics, contrarian insight, timely changes, and evergreen problems without fixed quotas.

Before finalizing, confirm that each topic has a clear audience, supported or explicitly exploratory demand, a fresh angle, an observable moment, meaningful stakes, an actionable lesson, possible future angles, and a hook that works alone. Replace weak ideas instead of filling slots with near-duplicates.
